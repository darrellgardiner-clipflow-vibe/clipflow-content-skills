# Set a posting rhythm they can keep

Use this when setting up a regular plan or reviewing whether the current one
still fits. For a one-off post, use the relevant context without making the
owner choose a whole publishing schedule.

## Keep three decisions separate

- **Cadence:** how often they intend to post on each account, over a week,
  fortnight or month. A monthly newsletter does not need a weekly slot.
- **Recurring slots:** what each regular post does for the audience, such as
  a buying question, a demonstration, a real result, a viewpoint or an offer.
- **This week's pieces:** the actual ideas and material chosen to fill those
  regular posts. A recurring customer-question post can answer a new question
  each week; it does not repeat the same script.

A recurring slot belongs to an account and channel. Give it a short stable
name or ID so it can be followed between weeks. One source can support posts
for different channels, but each version needs a useful job and its own fields.
Count the extra writing, editing and review work rather than treating cross-posting
as free.

## Start from their actual commitments

Read the brief, existing calendar and earlier plans first. Keep approved posts
and the current owner of the calendar. Use the goal, channels, available source
material, filming/editing time and review capacity they already gave you. Ask
only for gaps that change the plan.

Keep an existing cadence unless they ask to change it or it clearly exceeds
capacity. Explain any proposed change rather than silently replacing their
routine with three posts a week. If no rhythm exists, propose a small provisional
one that fits this business. State the assumptions; daily posting is not a
requirement, and the fallback count is not a commitment.

Store the rhythm in the **Posting rhythm** section of `content/brief.md` and
show the current week's choices in `content/week-plan.md`. Record account,
frequency/cycle, recurring role, optional day/window, source needs and review
date. Use the owner's timezone when assigning dates or times. If the precise
time is not needed, leave it open rather than claiming an ideal posting hour.

For example, one business may answer a buying question on Tuesday and show a
work process on Friday. Another may publish one useful explanation each fortnight.
Choose roles for their audience and goal; don't impose the same mix everywhere.

## Fill the week from supported ideas

Read `ideas.md` before looking for new ideas. Pick a piece whose source and
reader payoff fit the recurring role. Use current, relevant unused material,
or update/adapt something older when it still helps. Recheck prices, offers,
permissions and dates before reusing it. Note prior use; don't present an exact
repost as a new story or count one event as several pieces of proof.

Keep a small reserve of usable ideas when the material supports it. Record
whether an idea is available, planned, produced or actually published, with
week/post references. Being selected for a plan is not proof it was published.
Carry unfinished ideas forward without erasing the reason they were held.

When a slot has no suitable material, name the missing item and the next action:
record a customer answer, obtain permission for a result, finish the promised
resource, or choose an available alternative. A format change does not create
missing proof. Leave the post open or propose a clearly labelled one-week swap;
never fabricate a result or publish filler to complete the calendar.

## Check the whole workload

Count finding material, writing, filming, editing, design, review and handoff.
Separate this week's remaining work from assets already complete. Approved
caption text is not a finished Instagram feed post without a suitable image or
video; mark that asset dependency. A carousel
estimate does not cover building slides unless it says so. Use the owner's
actual times when available; otherwise make the estimates provisional.

If the plan exceeds capacity, preserve existing approved work and explain the
trade-off. Reduce or defer output, use an appropriate ready asset, or propose
additional help/time. Don't quietly increase the owner's filming or review load.
Batch shared setup when useful, but don't imply that a missing asset is ready.

## Review consistency without chasing a streak

Consistency also means recognizable topics, author voice and useful recurring
series. Keep what gives the audience a reason to return, while varying the
questions and examples. Do not force every post into the same opening or CTA.

At a sensible review point, compare planned, produced and actually published
posts, the work they took and the reasons any were missed. Use the goal's real
results when available; views alone do not determine whether the rhythm works.
Keep observations, possible explanations and proposed changes separate. With no
results, review feasibility and follow-through without inventing performance.

Retain a rhythm that fits. Propose a smaller one when repeated gaps show a
capacity or source problem, and increase only when the business wants to and
has the material and capacity. Update the shared rhythm when a change is agreed;
a single busy week can be an exception rather than a permanent new rule.

Planning dates and recurring slots never schedule or publish a post. Only a
requested action through the supported Clipflow connector can do that.
